# Writing Style — The Dan Shipper Method

Structure is for machines. Language is for humans. This guide codifies the writing techniques of Dan Shipper (Every.to) — articles that read like a smart friend sharing a discovery over coffee, while quietly satisfying every SEO and GEO requirement underneath.

**The core principle:** Write like a builder who just came back from the front line — with data, with scars, with thoughts about the bigger picture.

## 1. Opening Technique — Three Moves

Every article opens with one of three techniques. First paragraph: 3 sentences max. Definition sentence (Phase 2) goes in paragraph 2.

**Move A: Personal micro-scene (preferred — use 50%+ of the time)**

Open with a sensory, specific personal experience that creates suspense. The reader must think "wait, where is this going?"

> "A few years ago, when I felt like I was grasping desperately for something in my life that I couldn't name, I used to get up past midnight to smell spices."

> "Every AI tool I used last year forgot me by tomorrow. My preferences, my writing style, the context I'd spent twenty minutes explaining — gone."

Rules: First person. Sensory detail. A hint of vulnerability or strangeness. Never generic.

**On sensory density beyond the opening:** Dan Shipper doesn't save his sensory detail for the first paragraph — it runs through the whole piece. In his vibe coding article, the trail mix appears mid-article. In the agent-native piece, the skyscraper metaphor carries physical weight ("bronze and glass facade that smells faintly of sandalwood") deep into the body. The effect is that every few paragraphs, the reader gets pulled out of abstraction and back into a physical world.

Notice the difference:
- "I spent time setting up ChatGPT memory." — information only
- "all I could do was nervously munch trail mix as Codex investigated yet another bug buried deep in a codebase that I didn't understand." — food + body + specific object + self-deprecation doing four jobs simultaneously

When you write an analytical paragraph, ask: is there a physical detail that could ground this? It doesn't need to be elaborate — "at my kitchen table at 11pm" beats "at the end of the day" every time.

**Move B: Poetic abstraction + sharp insight (use ~30%)**

A philosophical observation that reframes the familiar, immediately followed by a pointed insight.

> "Time isn't as linear as you think. It has ripples and folds like smooth silk."

**Move C: Provocative assertion (use ~20%)**

A bold, contrarian claim that challenges received wisdom.

> "I hate to be the bearer of bad news, but all of the time we've spent organizing our notes was probably wasted."

**Never open with:** "In this article, we'll discuss..." / "X is becoming increasingly important..." / "In today's rapidly evolving landscape..." / A dictionary definition.

## 2. Sentence Rhythm — The Core Signature

Dan Shipper's most distinctive trait: the **long-build → short-punch → single-word bomb** pattern.

**How it works:**
- Stack 2-3 medium-to-long sentences (15-25 words) that build toward a point
- Follow with a short verdict sentence (2-5 words): "It works." / "That's the point." / "It's not." / "They will."
- Occasionally drop a single-word sentence for maximum impact: "Cumin." / "Gone." / "Success!"

**Target distribution:**
- Short sentences (<10 words): 25-30% — for verdicts, transitions, emotional peaks
- Medium sentences (10-25 words): 45-50% — the narrative workhorse
- Long sentences (>25 words): 20-25% — for detailed descriptions, cumulative lists

**Every analytical passage must end with a verdict sentence.** Don't let a section trail off. Land the plane in 2-5 words.

## 3. Paragraph Patterns

**Short paragraphs, always.** Average 2-3 sentences. 30%+ of all paragraphs should be single-sentence paragraphs.

Single-sentence paragraphs are used for:
- Transitions: "Here's what I mean by that."
- Emotional peaks: "Reading this almost made me cry."
- Assertions: "AI changes this equation."
- Pivots: "But that doesn't work."

**Visual rhythm:** Alternate short → medium → short → long → short. The eye should flow downward without getting stuck. No wall-of-text blocks.

## 4. Transitions — Conversational, Not Academic

Use transitions that sound like someone talking to you across a table:

**Oral-style transitions (preferred):**
- "Let's talk about what that looks like."
- "Here's what I mean by that."
- "Let me give you an example."
- "Here's where it gets interesting."

**Question transitions:**
- "But what happens when...?"
- "So how does this actually work?"
- "What's going on here?"

**Short pivot words:**
- "But." as a paragraph opener (frequently)
- "So,"
- "The problem is..."
- "This is where..."

**Never use:** "Furthermore" / "Moreover" / "In addition" / "It's worth noting" / "Additionally" / "That being said" — these belong in academic papers, not in articles people want to read.

## 5. Person & Authority — Never Preach

**Person switching pattern:**
- Narrative passages → "I" (personal experience, stories)
- Analytical passages → "You" and "We" (pull the reader in)
- Conclusions → back to "I" (personal reflection)

This creates an arc: "I experienced this → let me help you understand it → here's what I take away."

**How to establish authority without lecturing:**
1. First expose your own confusion or failure: "Recently, I've felt a little lost in my writing."
2. Then share an accidental discovery: "On a whim, I threw the list into ChatGPT..."
3. Let the result speak for itself: "Reading this almost made me cry."

**Never:** "You should..." / "It's crucial that you..." / "One must..." — the reader arrives at the conclusion themselves.

**On vulnerability and failure moments:** Dan Shipper doesn't use failure as a rhetorical device — he reports it. In his vibe coding piece, he writes "It felt less like programming and more like being the dumbest participant at a math Olympiad." In the agent-native piece: "my skyscrapers are shoddy. I start before the blueprint is final; I dig a foundation and sink some beams, but they are usually off by an eighth of an inch."

Notice what these moments do: they make the expertise land harder. You trust someone's conclusions more when you've seen where they've been lost. An article that only demonstrates competence reads like a brochure. An article that shows the struggle first — and the insight earned from it — reads like someone who actually lived through something.

The invitation isn't to manufacture confessions. It's to notice which part of the topic you genuinely found confusing, expensive, or embarrassing, and say so before explaining what you learned.

## 6. Voice Toolkit

| Technique | How to Use | Example |
|-----------|-----------|---------|
| **Parenthetical aside** | Self-deprecating humor inside parentheses — lowers authority, builds intimacy | "(I was not very good at this when I started.)" / "(I got a B in that class, so my understanding was pretty average.)" |
| **Em-dash (sparingly)** | Only for genuine dramatic pauses or mid-sentence pivots. **Max 2-3 per 1000 words.** Overuse is the #1 AI writing tell. When tempted, try a period, comma, colon, or parentheses instead. | "The result surprised me — it was better than anything I'd written that week." |
| **Italic emphasis** | Single-word emphasis for special meaning — not whole phrases | "He just *loved* wine" / "things that have what Robin calls *sparkle*" |
| **Precise numbers** | Replace every vague adjective with a specific number | "saves 15 hours per week" not "saves a lot of time" / "$2,000 in two days" not "a significant cost" |
| **Confessional honesty** | Moments where the author drops the expert mask | "The uncomfortable truth:" / "Honestly, it's messy." / "I don't have a clean answer for this one." |
| **Direct dialogue** | Insert quotes as if in conversation, not with academic attribution | 'I whispered "Deeply analytical" to my laptop.' not 'As noted by the sommelier (Johnson, 2024)...' |
| **"Let's..." invitations** | Pull the reader into the exploration together | "Let's break this down." / "Let's see how things go." |

**Vivid verbs — the silent upgrade:**
Dan Shipper never "writes" a document. He "hammers it out." He doesn't "get" an idea. He "snatches" it. The difference is physical texture. Replace generic verbs with verbs that carry sensory weight:

- "wrote" → "hammered out" / "sketched" / "scrawled"
- "used" → "reached for" / "grabbed" / "leaned on"
- "liked" → "loved" / "craved" / "couldn't put down"
- "showed" → "revealed" / "surfaced" / "exposed"
- "helped" → "rescued" / "unlocked" / "untangled"

**Rule:** In every paragraph, check the main verb. If it's a "be/have/get/make/do" default, swap it for something with muscle. Not every verb needs to be exotic, but zero vivid verbs per section is a tell.

## 7. Metaphor Rules

**Draw from these domains:**
- Body & senses: taste, smell, touch, physical sensations
- Food & cooking: recipes, ingredients, kitchen processes ("lamination" for layered writing)
- Everyday life: school exams, hiring, apartment hunting, cooking dinner
- Human relationships: trust-building, onboarding a new colleague, teaching a child

**Never draw from:**
- Business jargon: synergy, paradigm shift, leverage, disrupt, pivot
- Silicon Valley buzzwords: revolutionary, game-changing, 10x
- Sports metaphors (overused and exclusionary)

**On dominant metaphor architecture:** Dan Shipper tends to pick one central image per article and let it do a lot of work. In "Agent-native Architectures," the entire piece runs on skyscraper → garden: "Any application you use daily is a bronze and glass facade towering 500 feet above the street... But AI enables a new kind of software, one that's more like growing a garden than it is building a skyscraper." That opening metaphor comes back in the closing, transformed. The article feels like a complete thought.

When an article has a dozen small metaphors (assistant, recipe, toolbox, compass...) but no single one that carries the piece, the writing feels scattered even if each individual image works. It's the difference between a building with a structural beam and a pile of wooden furniture.

One approach: before writing, ask what single image from everyday life captures the *central tension* of the topic. Then let that image introduce itself early, recur at key turns, and close the piece. The other metaphors can still exist — they just don't carry weight.

**Technical concept rule:** Always explain through the human-relationship lens first. "An AI agent is less like a tool and more like a new hire. It needs onboarding, makes mistakes at first, and earns your trust one kept promise at a time." Then give the precise technical definition in a follow-up sentence or parenthetical.

**The three-step technical explanation:**
1. Everyday analogy: "Think of embeddings as a fingerprint for each chunk of text"
2. Minimal definition (parenthetical or follow-up sentence): "(a condensed mathematical representation that captures meaning)"
3. Immediate concrete example: show an actual input → output

Never let a technical concept hover in abstraction for more than one paragraph.

## 8. Section Openings — Feel Before Learn

Every major H2 section opens with one of these:
- A **concrete scenario** the reader has lived: "Think about the last time you onboarded a new colleague..."
- A **sensory micro-scene**: "Picture your email inbox at 7 AM on a Monday..."
- A **provocative question**: "What if the way we've been thinking about this is backwards?"

Then — and only then — move into the explanation.

**The rule:** The reader should feel something before learning something. Emotion opens the door; information walks through it.

## 9. Article Architecture — The Narrative Arc

Within the SEO structure (Phase 3's H2 skeleton stays intact), the overall narrative follows a four-part arc:

| Part | % of article | What happens |
|------|-------------|-------------|
| Personal entry | 20-25% | A specific experience, confusion, or problem the author faced |
| Concept elevation | 20-25% | Extract a bigger insight from the personal story |
| Evidence & method | 30-40% | Technical details, data, case studies, comparisons |
| Personal return | 10-15% | Circle back to the opening with a transformed perspective |

**The "aha moment"** lands at 40-60% of the article — the transition point from personal story to bigger concept. This is where the reader thinks "oh, I see what this is really about."

**How this interacts with H2 structure:** The arc applies to the *overall article narrative*. Each H2 section is a self-contained unit for SEO/GEO, but the emotional throughline connects them. Think of it as: H2 structure = skeleton, narrative arc = circulatory system.

**When to lead with verdict vs. when to let insight be earned:**
This is the central tension between SEO (scanners want the answer *now*) and great writing (readers want to *arrive* at the answer). The rule:
- **At the H2 section level:** Lead with verdict. A scanner who reads only the first sentence of any section should get the core point. This serves SEO and Progressive Disclosure.
- **Within the overall article arc:** Let the insight be earned. The opening personal story doesn't reveal the thesis. The "aha moment" at 40-60% is the payoff. A committed reader experiences a journey.
- **In practice:** Each H2 section opens with a verdict sentence, but the *sequence* of sections tells a story that builds toward deeper understanding. Verdict-first at the micro level, earned-insight at the macro level.

## 10. Endings — Circle Back, Don't Summarize

**On placement:** The closing paragraph comes *after* the FAQ section and Related Reading links, just before the SEO Meta block. It's easy for the ending to get buried — the FAQ section feels like a natural stopping point, and many drafts simply stop there. Dan Shipper's pieces don't. The last thing a reader touches is an image, not an answer.

**Preferred: Circular closure.** Return to the opening scene, but the author (and reader) have been changed by the journey.

If the article opened with "I used to get up past midnight to smell spices," the ending might return to that kitchen — but now the author understands something they didn't before.

> "That night I slept better than I had in years."

Notice what this does: it rewards the committed reader. The scanner got their answer in the first paragraph. The reader who stayed gets the emotional resolution.

**Alternative: Insight gold sentence.** A single sentence that crystallizes the entire article into something quotable and memorable.

> "The ultimate tool for thought is tools that think."

**Alternative: Warm encouragement.** A genuine, non-generic invitation to act.

> "I hope this inspires you to embark on that journey."

**What not to do:**

> "In conclusion, we've explored X, Y, and Z..." — this tells the reader you ran out of ideas and started recapping.

> *[Article ends at the last FAQ answer]* — this is an unfinished building. The FAQ is load-bearing; the ending is the door.

The closer you are to the opening scene at the end, the more the article feels like a complete thought rather than a document.

## 11. What to AVOID — The Blacklist

**Banned phrases:**
- "In today's rapidly evolving landscape of..."
- "It's important to note that..."
- "Let's dive into..." / "Without further ado..."
- "Furthermore" / "Moreover" / "In addition" / "Additionally"
- "It's worth noting that..."
- "You should..." (preachy)
- "Obviously" / "clearly" (implies reader is ignorant)
- "Revolutionary" / "game-changing" / "disruptive" (empty hype)
- "Powerful, intuitive, and seamless" (three-adjective lists)
- Any sentence that could appear in a press release

**Banned patterns:**
- Exclamation marks in body text
- Semicolons (too formal — use periods or commas instead)
- Academic citation format: "According to Smith (2024)..." — embed the source in narrative: "I learned this from Robin Sloan during an interview last year..."
- Summarizing what you just said at the end of a section
- Starting a section with its topic as a statement: "Memory is an important feature." — instead, open with a scene that *shows* why memory matters
